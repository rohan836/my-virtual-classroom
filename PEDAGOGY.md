# Classroom pedagogy — all subjects

## Audience and scope
Teach Indian ATL students in Grades 5–9. Assume a first-time learner at Grade 5 level unless a grade or prior knowledge is specified. Use everyday English, short sentences and familiar objects. Build understanding, not vocabulary memorization. One lesson has one central objective and a small set of observable outcomes. Teach prerequisite ideas just in time; defer unrelated advanced topics. Respect a requested duration; otherwise use 45 minutes (35 minutes of teaching, discussion and experiments, 10 minutes of notebook writing). A 40–50 minute lesson should work with normal class discussion, not silent reading or artificial padding.

## The teaching sequence
1. Begin with a familiar observation, puzzle or object and explain what students will understand.
2. Explain each prerequisite word and idea before using it. Establish intuition, then give a precise, age-appropriate definition.
3. Explain what the concept is, why it exists or is useful, how it works in simple steps, and where it appears in real life.
4. Carry one strong everyday example through the lesson. Show the analogy's correspondence and, when necessary, its limits. Add another example only when it clarifies a distinct point or counterexample.
5. Show the mechanism with a meaningful animated visual: before, during and after. Explain the cause of each visible change. Do not merely flash icons or reveal a label.
6. Let students predict, change a meaningful input, observe the result and compare with their prediction. Explain correct and incorrect outcomes and why they occurred.
7. Address common misconceptions and relevant limitations or safety considerations. Reinforce what the concept is not.
8. Check understanding with a few questions that ask for reasoning and show the answer and why. Assessment supplements teaching; it never replaces it.
9. End with a one-page notebook summary and one clear takeaway.

## Each explanation beat
Use a numbered question or statement as the heading, but make the explanation visible by default. Provide a direct 1–3 sentence answer, one concise example, a matching visual scene and its caption. Add a short discussion prompt with a revealable reason or follow-up. Students must be able to understand without an external search or an additional teacher guide. Sequence beats from easy to difficult. When a question is answered, explain the mechanism or reason, not just yes/no. Use more beats rather than dense paragraphs. Distinguish the main explanation from optional depth. The approved reference has 10 slides and 26 beats; this is a quality and pacing example, not a fixed quota. As a planning heuristic, 8–12 slides with 2–4 beats per teaching slide may suit a 45-minute class; adapt to actual content.

## Word explorer
Every new subject-specific or unfamiliar prerequisite term should be clickable wherever it matters, including examples, answers and notes. A popup provides the word, a one- or two-sentence meaning, a concrete example and a matching illustration or highlighted part where useful. Include a word bank accessible from any slide. Avoid circular definitions and new unexplained jargon inside definitions. A student should not need to ask what a necessary term means before continuing.

## Subject-specific depth
- AI: show inputs, examples, meaningful changes, outputs and limitations when relevant. Do not equate all AI with machine learning, claim all AI thinks like people, or misrepresent a fixed demo as a trained system. Label toy models and clarify what they actually compute. Teach only the AI concepts needed for the topic.
- Python: begin with the task and intuition; animate execution, values and outputs. Show editable or runnable code when code is the topic. Use tiny examples, explain each new symbol, and include actual output and a safe error case. Do not require programming for an introductory non-programming concept.
- Electronics: show what the component does, why it is used, how it behaves in a circuit and safe limits. Use labelled diagrams, cutaways and current/voltage visualization where useful. Distinguish conventional current from electron motion; explain simplifications, units and formula symbols before using them. Prefer safe low-voltage simulations and never imply live mains experimentation is appropriate.
- Robotics: connect sensing, decisions and motion with visible cause and effect. Show the input, controller and output, and allow safe changes to a parameter or condition. Explain real limitations without introducing irrelevant theory.

## Notebook and language
Reserve about 10 minutes by default. Use roughly 80–150 words where suitable, short numbered definitions, one example, a simple comparison or labelled sketch if useful and one takeaway. Include only material already taught. No quiz-only or decorative notes; do not repeat the whole lesson. Keep page content student-facing: no author, assistant, production notes, or requests to visit external sites. If teacher notes are explicitly requested, provide them separately with the exact same question IDs and headings.

## Acceptance criteria
A learner can explain the meaning, purpose and basic working of the concept in their own words, identify an example, complete an experiment, and explain why an outcome occurs. The lesson includes enough structured explanation to support its planned duration, but no filler. Every essential technical claim is accurate and the illustration, explanation and simulation agree.
