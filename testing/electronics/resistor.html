Slowing the Rush of Electric Current

Atal Tinkering Lab (Grades 5–9) · Carbon-Film Resistor, 4-Band Color Code & LED Safety Circuit
Summary

Electricity wants to race through a wire as fast as it can. Connect an LED straight to a 5 V battery and too much current rushes through, burning it out in a flash. A resistor acts like a narrow winding hallway inside the wire — slowing electric charge down to a safe, steady flow.

Key Equation · Ohm's Law

Current depends on battery push (Voltage, V) divided by opposition (Resistance, R): I = V ÷ R. Double the resistance and the current drops in half.

Introduction · fig. 01

Why every circuit needs a speed limit

Imagine opening a dam with no narrow channel to slow the water down: everything downstream gets flooded. Electric charge behaves the same way. A copper wire has almost zero resistance, so a battery pushes a huge rush of electrons through it.

To protect delicate parts like LEDs and microcontrollers, engineers place a resistor in the path. In fig. 01 below, switch between the zoomed-in 3D resistor and the full LED safety circuit to see how changing resistance controls the speed of flowing charge.

Section 1 · fig. 02

Inside a carbon-film resistor

From the outside, a resistor looks like a tiny tan peanut with colored stripes. Cut open the protective ceramic paint (Cutaway or X-Ray mode in the 3D stage below) and you will see four distinct layers:

1. Tinned copper leads plug into your breadboard (resistors have no + or − polarity). 2. Metallic end caps press onto a 3. white insulating ceramic core. 4. A laser-cut carbon spiral wraps around the core — more spiral turns make the path longer and skinnier, raising the resistance in Ohms (Ω).

Section 2 · fig. 03

Reading the 4-band color code

A resistor is too small to print numbers clearly, so manufacturers paint four colored stripes around its body. Read them from the side where the bands are packed closer together:

Band 1 gives the first digit, Band 2 gives the second digit, Band 3 multiplies by a power of 10 (how many zeros to add), and Band 4 shows tolerance (±5% Gold or ±10% Silver). For example, Red – Red – Brown means 22 × 10 = 220 Ω.

Ohm's Law & standard classroom values

When a resistor is wired in series with a red LED (which uses about 2.0 V), the remaining voltage pushes current through the resistor: I = (Vsupply − 2.0 V) ÷ R.

10–20 mA
Safe current for a standard 5 mm red LED
220 Ω
Go-to resistor for a 5 V Arduino / USB LED
> 35 mA
Danger zone: LED overheats and burns out
